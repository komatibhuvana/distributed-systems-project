                                              Abstract

          Cloud message brokers such as AWS SQS, RabbitMQ, and Apache Kafka serve as criti-
     cal infrastructure components enabling asynchronous communication in modern distributed
     systems. Accurate performance modeling of these systems is essential for capacity planning,
     service level agreement compliance, and cost optimization. The foundational work of Li et
     al. [1] introduced a tandem queue model for cloud message brokers using Colored Petri Nets
     with M/M/N queueing assumptions. However, this model relies on exponential service time
     distributions, which fail to capture the heavy-tailed behavior observed in real cloud work-
     loads. This paper extends the Li et al. framework by incorporating Pareto-distributed service
     times through M/G/N queueing models. We develop an enhanced analytical and simulation
     framework using Python and SimPy that supports heavy-tailed service time distributions,
     advanced M/G/N approximations including the Kingman, Whitt, and Allen-Cunneen meth-
     ods, and tandem queue configurations with traffic amplification from message retransmis-
     sions. Our simulation validates the base paper’s traffic inflation formula Λ2 = λ/(1 − p) to
     within 0.2% error while revealing that standard M/M/N models underestimate P99 latency
     by approximately 45% when workloads exhibit Pareto-distributed service times. We fur-
     ther demonstrate that Gaussian-based tail approximations are inadequate for heavy-tailed
     scenarios and propose Extreme Value Theory as a more accurate alternative for tail risk
     estimation. The framework provides interactive engineering tools through a React frontend
     and FastAPI backend for real-time capacity planning and performance analysis.

Keywords: Heavy-Tailed Distribution, Pareto Distribution, P99 Latency, Message Brokers,
Tandem Queue, M/G/N Approximation



1   Introduction
Cloud message brokers serve as the backbone of modern distributed systems, enabling reliable
asynchronous communication between services. Systems such as AWS SQS, RabbitMQ, Apache



                                                  1
Kafka, and Azure Queue Storage collectively handle billions of messages daily across global in-
frastructure deployments. The performance characteristics of these systems directly impact
application responsiveness, user experience, and operational costs. Accurate performance mod-
eling is therefore critical for determining server capacity requirements, ensuring compliance
with service level agreements such as P99 latency guarantees below 100 milliseconds, optimiz-
ing infrastructure costs by avoiding over-provisioning, and understanding how failure-induced
retransmissions affect system behavior under load.
    Traditional queueing models based on the M/M/N framework assume exponential service
times, which possess convenient mathematical properties including memorylessness and finite
variance. However, extensive empirical evidence from major cloud providers demonstrates that
real-world service times exhibit heavy-tailed behavior that deviates significantly from expo-
nential assumptions. As shown in Table 1, the discrepancy between M/M/N predictions and
observed Pareto-distributed behavior is substantial across multiple performance metrics.

    The objectives of this paper are fivefold. First, we reproduce and validate the base paper’s
M/M/N analytical results through discrete-event simulation. Second, we extend the model to
support heavy-tailed Pareto service time distributions using M/G/N approximations. Third,
we quantify the “reality gap” between classical exponential assumptions and heavy-tailed be-
havior in terms of mean latency, P50, and P99 metrics. Fourth, we evaluate the accuracy of
three established M/G/N approximation methods against simulation. Fifth, we demonstrate
the inadequacy of Gaussian-based P99 estimation and propose Extreme Value Theory as an
alternative.

1.1   Background and Literature Survey
The study of queueing systems has a rich history dating back to Erlang’s pioneering work
on telephone exchange modeling [2]. Kleinrock [3] established the theoretical foundations for
analyzing computer communication networks using queueing theory, while Gross and Harris
[4] provided comprehensive treatments of queueing system fundamentals that remain standard
references. Little’s Law, which relates the average number of customers in a system to the arrival
rate and average time spent, was formally proven by Little [5] and serves as a cornerstone of
queueing analysis.
    The recognition that real-world network traffic exhibits self-similar and heavy-tailed charac-
teristics was a watershed moment in performance modeling. Crovella and Bestavros [6] demon-
strated that World Wide Web traffic displays self-similarity caused by heavy-tailed file size
distributions, fundamentally challenging the Poisson arrival assumptions prevalent in earlier
models. Leland et al. [7] provided early statistical evidence of self-similar traffic patterns in
Ethernet networks, while Paxson and Floyd [8] extended these findings to wide-area network
traffic.


                                                2
    In the context of cloud computing, Dean and Barroso [9] published their influential “Tail
at Scale” paper demonstrating that P99 latencies dominate user experience in large-scale dis-
tributed systems. They showed that even rare slow responses become common when aggregated
across thousands of servers. DeCandia et al. [10] documented heavy-tailed response time distri-
butions in Amazon’s Dynamo key-value store, while the Netflix technology team [11] reported
Pareto-distributed latencies in their microservice architecture.
    For M/G/N queue approximations, Kingman [12] developed the heavy-traffic approximation
that adjusts M/M/N wait times using the coefficient of variation of service times. Whitt [13]
proposed refined approximations for GI/G/m queues that account for both inter-arrival and
service time variability. Allen [14] presented the Allen-Cunneen approximation, which provides
an alternative correction factor for general service time distributions. Marchal [15] contributed
bounds and approximations for multi-server queues that complement these methods.
    Extreme Value Theory has emerged as a powerful tool for tail risk estimation in computing
systems. Coles [16] provided the statistical foundations for modeling extreme values, while
Beirlant et al. [17] extended these methods to heavy-tailed distributions commonly encountered
in network traffic analysis. Resnick [18] developed the theoretical framework connecting heavy-
tailed phenomena to extreme value distributions.
    The base paper by Li et al. [1] introduced a tandem queue model for cloud message brokers
using Colored Petri Nets. Their model captures the two-stage pipeline architecture of message
broker systems and derives a traffic inflation formula that accounts for message retransmissions
due to delivery failures. While their work validated the model against M/M/N analytical so-
lutions, it assumed exponential service times throughout, leaving the impact of heavy-tailed
distributions unexplored. Harchol-Balter [19] provided extensive analysis of how heavy-tailed
job sizes affect queueing system performance, demonstrating that standard models can severely
underestimate tail latencies. Nair et al. [20] studied the impact of heavy tails on cloud ser-
vice performance, confirming that Pareto-distributed service times are common in production
environments.
    Our work builds directly on Li et al.’s tandem queue framework while addressing its key
limitation by incorporating heavy-tailed service time distributions and evaluating the resulting
impact on tail latency estimation.


2    The Tandem Queue Model
The tandem queue model captures the two-stage architecture of cloud message broker systems
where messages flow sequentially from a sender stage to a receiver stage. In this architecture,
producers submit messages to Stage 1, which handles initial processing including validation, se-
rialization, and routing. Messages that complete Stage 1 processing proceed to Stage 2, which
manages delivery to consumers including deserialization, acknowledgment handling, and persis-
tence. When a message delivery fails at Stage 2 with probability p, the message is retransmitted,
creating a feedback loop that amplifies the effective traffic arriving at Stage 2.
    Figure 1 illustrates the complete tandem queue architecture. The diagram shows the two
processing stages connected in series, with the feedback path for failed messages clearly indi-
cated. Stage 1 operates with N1 = 10 servers each processing at rate µ1 = 10 messages per
second, while Stage 2 operates with N2 = 8 servers at rate µ2 = 12 messages per second. The
external arrival rate is λ = 80 messages per second, and the failure probability is p = 0.1.

The asymmetry between stages reflects real-world broker deployments where the receiver stage
typically has fewer but faster servers due to the simpler nature of delivery operations compared
to ingestion.

3.1   M/M/N Baseline
The M/M/N queueing model assumes Poisson arrivals and exponential service times with N
identical servers. The server utilization is defined as ρ = λ/(N · µ), where λ is the arrival rate
and µ is the per-server service rate [3]. The system is stable when ρ < 1, meaning the aggregate
service capacity exceeds the arrival rate. The traffic intensity, defined as a = λ/µ, represents
the offered load measured in Erlangs [2].

The mean queue wait time follows directly from the Erlang-C probability, and Little’s Law [5]
relates the average number of customers in the system to the arrival rate and mean sojourn
time.

4    Heavy-Tailed Behavior and the Reality Gap
The distinction between exponential and Pareto tail behavior has profound implications for
performance modeling. An exponential distribution’s tail decays rapidly, making extreme values
exceedingly rare. In contrast, a Pareto distribution’s tail follows a power law, meaning extreme
values occur with much higher probability. Specifically, the probability of observing a value
exceeding 10 times the mean is 0.0000454 for an exponential distribution but 0.0316 for a
Pareto distribution with α = 2.5, making such extreme events approximately 696 times more
likely under heavy-tailed conditions.
    This phenomenon manifests in cloud message queues as the well-known Pareto principle:
a small fraction of messages consumes a disproportionate share of processing resources. In
practice, approximately 20% of messages account for 80% of total processing time, 1% of requests
generate roughly 50% of tail latency, and rare “straggler” messages dominate P99 performance
metrics. These observations are consistent with the findings of Dean and Barroso [9], who
demonstrated that tail latencies become increasingly problematic as system scale grows.
    Figure 4 presents a four-panel analysis of the heavy-tail impact on queue performance for
an M/G/N queue with λ = 100 msg/s, N = 10 threads, and µ = 12 msg/s/thread. Panel
(A) shows mean wait time decreasing as the Pareto shape parameter α increases from 2.1 to
4.0, confirming that heavier tails (lower α) produce longer average waits. Panel (B) plots P99
latency for both wait time and total response time against α, revealing that tail latency is far
more sensitive to the shape parameter than mean latency, with P99 response time reaching
nearly 0.5 seconds at α = 2.1. Panel (C) displays the squared coefficient of variation Cs2 as a
function of α, showing the rapid increase in service time variability as α approaches 2.0, where
variance becomes infinite. The red dashed line at Cs2 = 1 marks the exponential distribution
baseline, below which the Pareto distribution has lower variability than exponential but still
exhibits heavy-tailed behavior. Panel (D) presents the response time CDF for four values of α,
with all curves smoothly increasing to 1.0 without crossovers. Lower α values produce CDFs
that rise more slowly, indicating a higher probability of extreme response times. Error bars in
panels (A) through (C) represent 95% confidence intervals from 10 independent replications.
These results collectively demonstrate that the Pareto shape parameter has a dramatic effect
on tail performance that standard M/M/N models fail to capture.

4.2   Tail Risk and Extreme Value Theory
The standard P99 approximation based on Gaussian assumptions computes W99 = µW +2.33σW
[4], which yields 57.5 ms for our parameters. However, the actual simulation P99 is 145.8 ms,
representing a 45% underestimation. This failure occurs because the Gaussian approximation
assumes that latency follows a normal distribution, where the tail probability decays as P (W >
w) ≈ 1 − Φ((w − µ)/σ). For Pareto-distributed latencies, the tail instead follows a power law
P (W > w) ∼ w−α , which decays far more slowly.

5.1   Simulation Framework
We implemented our simulation framework using SimPy 4.1.1 [21], a process-based discrete-
event simulation library for Python. The backend exposes simulation capabilities through a
FastAPI REST API, while a React 18 frontend provides interactive visualization tools. Sta-
tistical analysis relies on NumPy and SciPy, with Matplotlib generating all figures. We chose
SimPy over the Colored Petri Net formalism used by Li et al. [1] because SimPy offers greater
flexibility in specifying arbitrary service time distributions, integrates naturally with Python’s
scientific computing ecosystem, and supports REST API integration for interactive analysis.
Algorithm 1 presents the pseudocode for our M/M/N queue simulation.

Algorithm 1 M/M/N Queue Simulation
 1: procedure        SimulateMMNQueue(arrival_rate,           service_rate,      num_servers,
    num_messages)
 2:    Initialize simulation environment
 3:    Create server pool with num_servers servers
 4:    Initialize empty list for wait times
 5:    for each message m ranging from 1 through num_messages do
 6:        Record arrival time of message m
 7:        Request a server from the pool
 8:        if no server is available then
 9:            Wait until a server becomes free
10:        end if
11:        Compute wait time as current time minus arrival time
12:        Generate service time from exponential distribution with rate service_rate
13:        Hold server for duration of service time
14:        Release server back to pool
15:        Record total sojourn time (wait plus service)
16:    end for
17:    return collected wait times for statistical analysis
18: end procedure

6    Conclusion and Future Work
This paper extended the tandem queue model of Li et al. [1] to address the critical limitation
of exponential service time assumptions in cloud message broker performance modeling. Our
simulation framework, built on Python and SimPy, successfully reproduced all key results from
the base paper, validating the traffic inflation formula Λ2 = λ/(1 − p) to within 0.2% error and
matching M/M/N analytical predictions within 2% across all metrics.
    The central finding of this work is the quantification of the “reality gap” between M/M/N
predictions and observed performance under Pareto-distributed service times. Standard models
underestimate P99 latency by approximately 45%, with the Gaussian-based P99 approximation
yielding 57.5 ms compared to the actual simulation value of 145.8 ms. This underestimation
has severe practical consequences for capacity planning and SLA compliance. We demonstrated
that Extreme Value Theory, specifically the Generalized Pareto Distribution, provides accurate
tail risk estimates where Gaussian methods fail.
    Among the three M/G/N approximation methods evaluated, the Whitt approximation
achieved the best overall accuracy with 5.1% mean wait error and 18.2% P99 error, making
it the recommended analytical method for practitioners who require quick estimates without
full simulation. However, for critical capacity planning decisions involving tail latency guaran-
tees, direct simulation with heavy-tailed distributions remains essential.
    Several directions for future work emerge from this research. First, incorporating real pro-
duction traces from cloud message brokers would validate the Pareto assumption and calibrate
the shape parameter α for specific systems. Second, extending the model to multi-region de-
ployments with geo-distributed queues would address an increasingly common architectural
pattern. Third, integrating machine learning-based prediction models could enable adaptive
capacity planning that responds to changing workload characteristics in real time. Fourth,
connecting the simulation framework to Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler would enable
automated scaling decisions based on heavy-tailed performance models rather than simple uti-
lization thresholds.
